Results and Discussion

Overview

We looked at 55 reports from the International monetary fund across 14 countries. Nine of these countries had crises. Five did not. We checked these reports for five things: how sustainable the country's finances are, how stable the banking system is, how urgent the situation is, how much things are getting worse and how vulnerable the country is to outside problems. We gave each of these things a score from 0 to 10. Then we saw if these scores could tell us which countries were in crisis and which were not. We also wanted to know if these scores could warn us about a crisis before it happens.

Crisis vs non Crisis years

When we compared reports from crisis years to non crisis years, the average scores looked higher for crisis years on most things. The biggest gap was in how vulnerable the country was to outside problems. Reports from crisis years gave an average score of 7.8 for this while non crisis years gave 4.7. Things getting worse and how urgent the situation was also looked higher in crisis years. The stability of the banking system was the one that looked most similar between the two. This might be because the International Monetary Fund talks about banking problems in a way that's similar for all countries or because they are careful with their words.

The early warning test

The more important test is whether reports from before a crisis already carry a warning. We looked at reports from one or two years before a crisis and compared them to calm years. Here only two things came out as clearly significant. The strongest was how vulnerable the country was to outside problems: 7.7 before a crisis versus 4.5 in calm years (p < 0.001). Things getting worse was also significant but weaker (p = 0.034). The other three (finances, banking, and urgency) were higher on average before a crisis but the difference was not strong enough to be sure given our small sample. So the clearest early warning sign in the IMF's language is about a country's vulnerability to outside problems.

Power

We also used each of the five things to try to predict which countries would have a crisis the next year. All five did better than chance. How vulnerable the country was to outside problems was the best predictor (AUC 0.87), followed by the stability of the banking system (0.78) and how urgent the situation was (0.76). It is interesting that banking was a decent predictor here even though its averages were not very different between crisis and non crisis years. The reason is that the two tests measure different things: one compares averages, the other looks at how well the scores can rank crisis years above calm ones.

Anchor case: Lebanon

Lebanon is a good example of how this works. Reports from 2015 already showed some warning signs, especially around the country's vulnerability to outside problems. These signs got stronger over time and were very high by the time the country had a crisis in 2019, staying high through 2023. This matches what we found in the wider analysis: outside vulnerability is the clearest warning sign of a coming crisis.

Interpretation and limitations

Our results show that the IMF's reports do carry some information that can help predict a crisis, especially around a country's vulnerability to outside problems. This is similar to what other researchers have found, since external imbalances are among the most reliable warning signs in the crisis literature. However our analysis has some limitations. We only looked at 14 countries and 55 reports, which is not a lot, so the test has limited power. We also did not try to figure out if the reports actually caused the crisis or just if they were related to it. The scores were generated by a computer program and might not be perfect. Finally the way we dated the crises combined Reinhart-Rogoff data with recent events we added by hand. Despite these limitations we think the results are reasonable and that the warning sign we found is real.
